A vehicle whose direct-to-cell satellite is hidden by street facades can pass its status report to neighbors over the sidelink. Which neighbors should forward it, and how many? In packet-level simulation of 638 satellites, one report per second, stop-and-go traffic and an assumed uplink of 300 kb/s per satellite, a one-bit uplink announcement in each status broadcast let a single named neighbor cut the median age of information from 1.19 and 3.63 to 0.53 and 0.58 s in an urban and a dense canyon, and the distance driven since the server’s newest report from 7.8 and 24.3 to 3.3 and 3.6 m. Among forwarding rules, differences were smaller and lay mostly in cost. Two announcing neighbors drawn at random were among the freshest rules, at 1.9 and 1.8 uplink forwards per blocked message; thinned every-decoder forwarding needed 4.5 to 4.9 to match them and unthinned forwarding overloaded the uplink. Two named neighbors paid while the offered uplink load stayed below about 0.78 of capacity and lost in the dense canyon once it passed one. A random announcing neighbor beat the nearest one at every start, since a near vehicle soon meets the same facade, as our model’s stale-flag term anticipates. From the facade-height law, the trajectories and each run’s mean delay, the model predicted the age of eight baseline rules with median errors of 23 and 20 % of the age above its floor. Forwarding is assumed at the application layer, and visibility rests on building footprints.
